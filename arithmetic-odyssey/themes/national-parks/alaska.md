## Denali National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Alaska |
| **Became a national park** | February 26, 1917 (as Mount McKinley National Park; enlarged and renamed Denali in 1980) |
| **Size** | about 4.74 million acres (7,410 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | about 543,000 (39th most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **The mountain:** North America's tallest peak (20,310 ft). It is often hidden by clouds, so seeing the summit is a matter of luck.
- **Denali Park Road:** A 92-mile road into the wilderness, mostly open only to park buses. A landslide at Pretty Rocks has stopped traffic at mile 43 since 2021 while a bridge is built.
- **Wildlife:** The "Big Five": grizzly bears, moose, caribou, Dall sheep and wolves.
- **Sled dog kennels:** The only national park with working sled dogs, which patrol the park in winter.

**History:** Athabascan peoples have lived in the region for thousands of years and call the mountain Denali, "the high one." Naturalist Charles Sheldon, who spent the winter of 1907–08 there, campaigned for a park to protect its Dall sheep and other wildlife from market hunters. It was created in 1917 as Mount McKinley National Park, then tripled in size and was renamed Denali by the 1980 Alaska lands act. The mountain itself was renamed Denali in 2015 and changed back to Mount McKinley by the federal government in 2025, though the park keeps the name Denali.

**Good to know:** The main season is late May to mid-September. Only the first 15 miles of the road are open to private cars.

## Gates of the Arctic National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Alaska |
| **Became a national park** | December 2, 1980 (national monument from 1978) |
| **Size** | about 7.52 million acres (11,750 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | about 15,000 (62nd most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **Untouched wilderness:** The northernmost national park, entirely north of the Arctic Circle, with no roads, trails or facilities.
- **Brooks Range:** Jagged peaks such as the granite spires of the Arrigetch Peaks.
- **Wild rivers:** Six Wild and Scenic Rivers, including the Noatak and the North Fork of the Koyukuk, popular for multi-week float trips.
- **Caribou:** Part of the range of the Western Arctic caribou herd, one of the largest in North America.

**History:** Iñupiat and Koyukon Athabascan peoples have lived and hunted here for thousands of years; the Nunamiut village of Anaktuvuk Pass lies inside the park. Forester and wilderness advocate Bob Marshall explored the area in the 1930s and named two peaks on either side of the North Fork Koyukuk the "gates" to the Arctic. The park was created by the 1980 Alaska lands act.

**Good to know:** Visitors fly in by air taxi from Fairbanks, Bettles or Coldfoot, or hike in from the Dalton Highway on the park's eastern edge.

## Glacier Bay National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Alaska |
| **Became a national park** | December 2, 1980 (national monument from 1925) |
| **Size** | about 3.22 million acres (5,040 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | about 740,000 (32nd most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **Tidewater glaciers:** Glaciers such as Margerie and Johns Hopkins that end in the sea and calve icebergs into the water.
- **Marine wildlife:** Humpback whales, sea otters, harbor seals, puffins and Steller sea lions.
- **Bartlett Cove:** The only developed area, with a lodge, forest trails and the Huna Tribal House, opened in 2016.
- **Kayaking and cruises:** Most visitors see the bay from cruise ships; kayakers can be dropped off in the wilderness.

**History:** Glacier Bay is the homeland of the Huna Tlingit, who were forced out by an advancing glacier centuries ago. When Captain George Vancouver passed in 1794 the bay was almost entirely ice; by the time John Muir visited in 1879, the ice had retreated about 48 miles, one of the fastest glacier retreats ever recorded. It became a national monument in 1925 and a national park in 1980, and is part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site shared with Canada.

**Good to know:** No roads reach the park. Visitors arrive by cruise ship, or by plane or ferry to the town of Gustavus.

## Katmai National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Alaska |
| **Became a national park** | December 2, 1980 (national monument from 1918) |
| **Size** | about 3.67 million acres (5,740 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | about 34,000 (59th most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **Brooks Falls:** Brown bears standing in the waterfall catching leaping sockeye salmon, best in July and September.
- **Fat Bear Week:** An online contest each fall where people vote for the fattest bear, watched on the park's live bear cams.
- **Valley of Ten Thousand Smokes:** A valley buried by ash in the 1912 Novarupta eruption, the largest volcanic eruption of the 20th century.
- **Coastal bears:** Large numbers of brown bears dig for clams and graze on sedge along the coast.

**History:** The Alutiiq (Sugpiaq) people lived along this coast for thousands of years. In 1912 the Novarupta eruption buried the area in ash; botanist Robert Griggs led National Geographic expeditions in 1916 and found a valley full of steaming vents, which led to the national monument in 1918. The 1980 Alaska lands act made it a national park, adding protection for its bears and salmon.

**Good to know:** Brooks Camp is reached by floatplane from King Salmon. Camping and lodge stays need reservations well ahead.

## Kenai Fjords National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Alaska |
| **Became a national park** | December 2, 1980 (national monument from 1978) |
| **Size** | about 669,000 acres (1,050 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | about 425,000 (45th most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **Harding Icefield:** An ice field of about 700 square miles that feeds nearly 40 glaciers.
- **Exit Glacier:** The only part of the park reachable by road; signs along the trail mark how far the glacier has retreated since 1815.
- **Boat tours:** Cruises from Seward into the fjords to see tidewater glaciers, orcas, humpback whales, sea lions and puffins.
- **Harding Icefield Trail:** A steep 8-mile round-trip hike to a view over the ice field.

**History:** The coast is the homeland of the Sugpiaq (Alutiiq) people. The fjords are glacial valleys flooded by the sea as the land sinks along a fault, and the 1964 Alaska earthquake dropped parts of the coast by several feet. The park was created by the 1980 Alaska lands act and is one of Alaska's most accessible national parks.

**Good to know:** Seward is about a 2.5-hour drive or a train ride from Anchorage. Boat tours run roughly May to September.

## Kobuk Valley National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Alaska |
| **Became a national park** | December 2, 1980 (national monument from 1978) |
| **Size** | about 1.75 million acres (2,740 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | 7,786 (63rd most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **Great Kobuk Sand Dunes:** Arctic sand dunes covering about 25 square miles, some up to 100 ft high, left from glaciers that ground rock into sand.
- **Caribou migration:** Hundreds of thousands of caribou of the Western Arctic herd cross the Kobuk River each spring and fall.
- **Onion Portage:** An archaeological site where people have hunted caribou at the river crossing for about 9,000 years.
- **Kobuk River:** A slow river for float trips through the boreal forest.

**History:** The Iñupiat have hunted caribou and fished here for thousands of years and still use the land. Excavations at Onion Portage, led by archaeologist J. Louis Giddings in the 1960s, revealed layers of human history going back about 9,000 years. The park was created by the 1980 Alaska lands act and is the least visited national park.

**Good to know:** There are no roads or facilities. Air taxis fly from Kotzebue or Bettles.

## Lake Clark National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Alaska |
| **Became a national park** | December 2, 1980 (national monument from 1978) |
| **Size** | about 2.62 million acres (4,090 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | about 20,000 (61st most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **Volcanoes:** Two active volcanoes, Redoubt and Iliamna, both over 10,000 ft.
- **Bear viewing:** Brown bears feeding on sedge and clams at Silver Salmon Creek and Chinitna Bay on the coast.
- **Proenneke's cabin:** The hand-built cabin of Richard Proenneke at Twin Lakes, made famous by the film "Alone in the Wilderness."
- **Lake Clark:** A 40-mile-long turquoise lake and salmon spawning grounds that feed Bristol Bay's fishery.

**History:** The area is the homeland of the Dena'ina Athabascan people; the village of Kijik near Lake Clark was occupied for centuries. Richard Proenneke built his log cabin at Twin Lakes in 1968 with hand tools and lived there for about 30 years, filming and writing about his life. The park was created by the 1980 Alaska lands act.

**Good to know:** No roads reach the park. Small planes fly from Anchorage, Homer or Kenai, about an hour away.

## Wrangell–St. Elias National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Alaska |
| **Became a national park** | December 2, 1980 (national monument from 1978) |
| **Size** | about 8.32 million acres (13,000 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | about 109,000 (55th most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **The largest national park:** The park and its preserve cover 13.2 million acres, larger than Switzerland.
- **Kennecott:** A preserved copper-mining town with a 14-story red mill building, a National Historic Landmark.
- **Root Glacier:** A glacier you can walk to from Kennecott and explore with a guide and crampons.
- **Giant peaks:** Mount St. Elias (18,008 ft), the second-highest peak in the US, and 9 of the 16 highest US peaks.

**History:** Ahtna and Upper Tanana Athabascan, Eyak and Tlingit peoples lived in this region. Rich copper ore found in 1900 led to the Kennecott mines and a 196-mile railroad to the coast; the mines closed in 1938, leaving a ghost town. The park was created in 1980 and is part of the largest internationally protected wilderness in the world, a UNESCO World Heritage Site shared with Canada.

**Good to know:** The 60-mile gravel McCarthy Road leads to McCarthy and Kennecott. The visitor center at Copper Center is about 4 hours from Anchorage.
