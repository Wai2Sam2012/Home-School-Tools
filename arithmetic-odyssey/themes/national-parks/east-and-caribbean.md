## Acadia National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Maine |
| **Became a national park** | February 26, 1919 (Sieur de Monts National Monument from 1916; called Lafayette National Park until 1929) |
| **Size** | about 49,100 acres (77 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | 4.08 million (7th most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **Cadillac Mountain:** The highest point on the US Atlantic coast (1,530 ft), one of the first places in the country to see sunrise for part of the year.
- **Park Loop Road:** A scenic drive past Sand Beach, Thunder Hole and Otter Cliff.
- **Carriage roads:** 45 miles of car-free gravel roads with stone bridges, built by John D. Rockefeller Jr.
- **Jordan Pond and Bass Harbor:** Popovers at the Jordan Pond House, and the Bass Harbor Head Lighthouse.

**History:** Mount Desert Island is the homeland of the Wabanaki peoples. French explorer Samuel de Champlain named the island in 1604. Wealthy summer residents bought much of the land in the late 1800s, and George B. Dorr and Charles Eliot led the effort to donate it to the public. It became the first national park east of the Mississippi River in 1919 and was renamed Acadia in 1929.

**Good to know:** A vehicle reservation is needed for the Cadillac Summit Road in the busy season. The free Island Explorer bus runs in summer.

## Biscayne National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Florida |
| **Became a national park** | June 28, 1980 (national monument from 1968) |
| **Size** | about 173,000 acres (270 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | about 487,000 (41st most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **Coral reefs:** Part of the only living coral barrier reef in the continental US, great for snorkeling and diving.
- **Maritime Heritage Trail:** A trail of shipwrecks you can explore underwater.
- **Boca Chita Key:** A small island with a historic ornamental lighthouse.
- **Islands and mangroves:** Mangrove shoreline, keys such as Elliott Key, and manatees.

**History:** About 95% of the park is water. The Tequesta people lived here, and later pirates and shipwreck salvagers roamed the reefs. In the 1960s developers planned a city on the islands and an oil refinery on the shore; local people fought back, and it became a national monument in 1968 and a national park in 1980. Lancelot Jones, who lived on Porgy Key, sold his family's land to the park instead of to developers.

**Good to know:** A boat is needed to see most of the park. Tours leave from the Dante Fascell Visitor Center near Homestead.

## Congaree National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | South Carolina |
| **Became a national park** | November 10, 2003 (national monument from 1976) |
| **Size** | about 26,700 acres (42 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | about 288,000 (49th most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **Old-growth forest:** The largest stretch of old-growth bottomland hardwood forest left in the southeastern US.
- **Champion trees:** Some of the tallest trees in eastern North America, and bald cypress "knees."
- **Boardwalk Loop:** An easy 2.6-mile boardwalk through the floodplain.
- **Synchronous fireflies and canoeing:** Fireflies that flash together in late spring, and the Cedar Creek canoe trail.

**History:** The Congaree people lived along the river. Later, people escaping slavery hid in the forest. Because frequent floods made logging hard, much of the forest survived, and when logging threatened it in the 1960s, activists such as Harry Hampton campaigned to save it. It became a national monument in 1976 and a national park in 2003.

**Good to know:** There is no entrance fee. The forest floods often; check trail conditions first.

## Dry Tortugas National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Florida |
| **Became a national park** | October 26, 1992 (Fort Jefferson National Monument from 1935) |
| **Size** | about 64,700 acres (100 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | about 89,000 (56th most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **Fort Jefferson:** The largest all-brick fort in the Americas, built with about 16 million bricks.
- **Snorkeling:** Coral, fish and sea turtles right off the beach and along the fort's moat wall.
- **Birds:** Thousands of sooty terns and brown noddies nest here each spring.
- **Loggerhead Key:** A lighthouse island with excellent snorkeling.

**History:** Spanish explorer Juan Ponce de León named the islands Las Tortugas ("the turtles") in 1513; sailors later added "Dry" because there is no fresh water. Fort Jefferson was built from 1846 to 1875 but never finished, and it served as a prison during and after the Civil War. Dr. Samuel Mudd, convicted after Lincoln's assassination, was held there. The park is about 99% water.

**Good to know:** The park is about 70 miles west of Key West, reached by ferry or seaplane.

## Everglades National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Florida |
| **Became a national park** | December 6, 1947 (authorized by Congress in 1934) |
| **Size** | about 1.51 million acres (2,360 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | about 778,000 (31st most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **River of Grass:** A slow-moving sheet of water flowing across sawgrass prairie.
- **Anhinga Trail:** A short walk with alligators, turtles and wading birds.
- **Shark Valley:** A 15-mile loop by tram or bike to an observation tower.
- **Alligators and crocodiles:** The only place in the world where both live side by side. Florida panthers and manatees live here too.

**History:** The Calusa and Tequesta peoples lived here, followed by the Seminole and Miccosukee. Drainage canals in the early 1900s began to dry out the wetlands. In 1947, the year the park was dedicated, Marjory Stoneman Douglas published "The Everglades: River of Grass," which changed how people saw the wetland. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and a huge restoration effort to bring water back has been under way since 2000.

**Good to know:** Visit in the dry season, December to April, when there are fewer mosquitoes and more wildlife at the water holes.

## Great Smoky Mountains National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Tennessee, North Carolina |
| **Became a national park** | June 15, 1934 (authorized by Congress in 1926) |
| **Size** | about 522,400 acres (820 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | 11.53 million (1st most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **Cades Cove:** A valley loop road with historic cabins, churches and lots of wildlife.
- **Kuwohi:** The highest point in the park and in Tennessee (6,643 ft), with an observation tower. It was called Clingmans Dome until 2024.
- **Trails and waterfalls:** About 70 miles of the Appalachian Trail, plus hikes such as Alum Cave and Laurel Falls.
- **Wildlife:** About 1,900 black bears, and so many salamander species that it is called the "Salamander Capital of the World."

**History:** The Great Smoky Mountains are the homeland of the Cherokee; most were forced west on the Trail of Tears in 1838, and the Eastern Band of Cherokee lives next to the park today. Logging stripped much of the forest in the early 1900s. To create the park, people and the states of Tennessee and North Carolina raised money to buy the land, matched by $5 million from the Rockefeller family; more than 1,000 families had to move. President Franklin Roosevelt dedicated the park in 1940. It is the most visited national park by far.

**Good to know:** There is no entrance fee, but a parking tag is needed to park for more than 15 minutes.

## Hot Springs National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Arkansas |
| **Became a national park** | March 4, 1921 (Hot Springs Reservation from 1832) |
| **Size** | about 5,600 acres (9 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | 2.49 million (14th most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **Bathhouse Row:** Eight historic bathhouses; you can still take a traditional bath at the Buckstaff.
- **Thermal springs:** Water about 143°F (62°C) that fell as rain about 4,000 years ago.
- **Fill a jug:** Public fountains where you can fill bottles with spring water.
- **Trails:** About 26 miles of hiking trails on the mountains above town.

**History:** Native peoples called the area the "Valley of the Vapors." In 1832 Congress set the hot springs aside as a federal reservation, 40 years before Yellowstone, making it the oldest area protected by the federal government that is now a national park. It became a famous spa town, a spring-training spot for baseball teams and a gangster hangout in the 1920s and 1930s. It became a national park in 1921.

**Good to know:** The park is in the middle of the city of Hot Springs. There is no entrance fee.

## Mammoth Cave National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Kentucky |
| **Became a national park** | July 1, 1941 (authorized by Congress in 1926) |
| **Size** | about 54,000 acres (84 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | about 661,000 (34th most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **The longest known cave system in the world:** About 426 miles of mapped passages, and still growing.
- **Cave tours:** Ranger-led tours such as the Historic Tour, Domes and Dripstones, and the crawling Wild Cave Tour.
- **Frozen Niagara:** A large flowstone formation that looks like a frozen waterfall.
- **Green River:** Canoeing, kayaking and hiking above ground.

**History:** People explored the cave about 5,000 years ago. During the War of 1812, enslaved workers mined saltpeter in the cave to make gunpowder. In the 1830s and 1840s, Stephen Bishop, an enslaved guide, explored and mapped many miles of the cave. It became a national park in 1941 and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

**Good to know:** The cave is about 54°F (12°C) all year, so bring a jacket. Book tours ahead in summer.

## New River Gorge National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | West Virginia |
| **Became a national park** | December 27, 2020 (national river from 1978; officially New River Gorge National Park and Preserve) |
| **Size** | about 73,000 acres (110 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | 1.96 million (17th most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **New River Gorge Bridge:** A 3,030-ft steel arch bridge, 876 ft above the river. On Bridge Day each October, people BASE jump off it.
- **Whitewater rafting:** Some of the best whitewater in the eastern US.
- **Rock climbing:** More than 1,400 climbing routes on the sandstone cliffs.
- **Coal towns:** Ruins of old mining towns such as Thurmond, Nuttallburg and Kaymoor.

**History:** Despite its name, the New River is thought to be one of the oldest rivers in North America. After the C&O Railway arrived in the 1870s, coal mining towns grew up along the gorge, then faded as the mines closed. The bridge opened in 1977, and the river became a national river in 1978. In 2020 it became the 63rd and newest national park. Most of its land is a preserve, where hunting is allowed.

**Good to know:** There is no entrance fee. The national park area itself is about 7,000 acres; the rest is preserve.

## Shenandoah National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Virginia |
| **Became a national park** | December 26, 1935 |
| **Size** | about 200,000 acres (310 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | 1.68 million (19th most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **Skyline Drive:** A 105-mile road along the crest of the Blue Ridge Mountains, with about 70 overlooks.
- **Old Rag:** A popular rock-scramble hike; a day-use ticket is needed for much of the year.
- **Waterfalls and peaks:** Dark Hollow Falls, and Hawksbill, the highest peak in the park (4,051 ft).
- **Appalachian Trail and fall colors:** About 100 miles of the Appalachian Trail, and bright fall leaves in October.

**History:** The Monacan and Manahoac peoples lived in these mountains. To create the park, Virginia bought land from about 500 families living there, many of whom were moved out, some against their will. The Civilian Conservation Corps built much of the park, and President Franklin Roosevelt dedicated it in 1936. The park's facilities were segregated until 1950.

**Good to know:** The park is about 75 miles from Washington, DC. Skyline Drive is busiest on October weekends.

## Virgin Islands National Park

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | US Virgin Islands (St. John) |
| **Became a national park** | August 2, 1956 |
| **Size** | about 15,000 acres (23 sq mi) |
| **Visitors in 2025** | about 471,000 (42nd most visited of 63) |

**Key attractions**

- **Beaches:** Trunk Bay, with an underwater snorkeling trail, and Cinnamon and Maho bays.
- **Coral reefs and sea turtles:** Snorkeling with sea turtles, especially at Maho Bay.
- **Annaberg Sugar Plantation:** Ruins of a sugar mill worked by enslaved Africans.
- **Reef Bay Trail:** A forest trail to petroglyphs carved by the Taíno people.

**History:** The Taíno and earlier peoples lived on St. John. Under Danish rule, the island was covered with sugar plantations worked by enslaved Africans, who led one of the first major slave revolts in the Americas in 1733. The US bought the islands from Denmark in 1917. Laurance Rockefeller bought much of St. John and donated it to create the park in 1956. The park covers about 60% of the island. Hurricanes Irma and Maria badly damaged it in 2017.

**Good to know:** Ferries run from St. Thomas to St. John.
